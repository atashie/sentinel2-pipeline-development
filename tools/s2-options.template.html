01 / DATA

Two public sensors. We start with Sentinel-2.

A satellite measures reflected sunlight in separate wavelength bands. A water-quality model reads those bands for signs of algae, sediment, and dissolved organic matter.

Chlorophyll and cyanobacteriaPigments shape the red and red-edge bands.
Turbidity and sedimentSuspended particles brighten red and near-infrared.
Dissolved organic matterTea-coloured water darkens blue and green.
Surface temperatureNeeds a thermal band. Sentinel-2 has none.

The modeling team decides which bands to use. The store keeps every band, so that decision can change later without re-ingesting.

Sentinel-2

OUR CHOICE
Pixel size
10 m in blue, green, red, and near-infrared. Nine samples where Landsat has one.
Revisit
Every five days at the equator with two satellites. Clouds decide how many are usable.
Red edge
Three bands between red and near-infrared, at 20 m. Chlorophyll estimates depend on them.
Consistency
One instrument design on all three satellites since 2015. Ground processing versions still change, and we track them.
Currency
New scenes appear in the AWS catalog within hours. No guarantee is stated.
Missing
No thermal band. No record before 2015.

Landsat

THE OTHER PUBLIC OPTION · NOT USED FOR NOW
Pixel size
30 m for the multispectral bands on Landsat 8 and 9. Fewer samples on a small lake.
Revisit
Every eight days with the two current satellites.
Red edge
None.
Consistency
A chain of different sensors since 1972. Landsat 6 never reached orbit. Landsat 7 lost a fifth of every scene to gaps after 2003. Landsat 8 and 9 are a matched pair.
Currency
Current, with two satellites in operation.
Strengths
Thermal bands at 100 m for surface temperature. A record back to 1972.

About 30 m Sentinel-2. Some colleagues know Sentinel-2 at 30 m through NASA’s Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 product. That product is derived from Sentinel-2 at 30 m on a Landsat-compatible grid, so the two share one time series. It is a different product from the 10 m archive we would read. It has nine times fewer pixels, a different atmospheric correction, and adjustments to match Landsat. Prototyping will confirm which product the model was built on.

The choice. Sentinel-2, for its finer pixels, more frequent revisit, red-edge bands, and one consistent instrument. Landsat waits until surface temperature or pre-2015 history is needed.

03 / QUALITY

This is where things can go wrong.

A change in a pixel value can come from the water, the atmosphere, or the processing. We need enough context to tell them apart. Six categories cover the main quality risks.

Land leaks into a water pixel.

Small ponds and shorelines produce mixed pixels. A small location error can change how much land enters a measurement.

Our approach: distinguish interior, shoreline, and nearby land. Keep the amount of water coverage.

@@RISK_LAND@@

Clear-looking water can be misleading.

Clouds, shadows, haze, sun glint, and nearby bright land can affect the signal. Quality flags help, but they are imperfect.

Our approach: preserve available quality flags and shoreline context. Test the screening rules.

@@RISK_FLAGS@@

The same lake can arrive twice.

Satellite tiles overlap. Processing can give the same location different values or quality labels in neighboring tiles.

Our approach: keep each tile’s values and identify a primary source. Measure differences before trusting a stitched view.

@@RISK_TILES@@

A processing change can look like a trend.

Archives mix processing versions and conventions for converting stored numbers into reflectance. Handling them wrongly creates false changes.

Our approach: retain product history and test value conversion across archive versions.

@@RISK_VERSIONS@@

A sharper grid can imply false detail.

Sentinel-2 bands have 10, 20, or 60 m pixels. Enlarging a coarse band does not create finer observations.

Our approach: preserve native band resolution. Make resampling explicit when an analysis combines grids.

@@RISK_GRID@@

A missing date can be misread.

A gap may mean missing coverage, a catalog omission, or an unusable observation. Those have different implications for a model.

Our approach: track coverage and quality separately. Preserve reasons for missing data.

@@RISK_MISSING@@

Documented: Reviewed geospatial evidence and processing practices. Project direction: Data content and provenance. The drawings are explanatory schematics. Screening effectiveness and cross-tile differences remain unmeasured.

04 / WORKLOAD

Read only what we need, and reuse the work.

We only need the pixels around water bodies. Five steps get us there. Prototyping will time them and compare methods.

ONCE PER WATER BODY · REDONE WHEN GEOMETRY CHANGES

Prepare the places.

Repeat when boundaries, grids, or classification rules change.

1

Locate water bodies on satellite grids.

Find the tiles and image windows that cover each lake and its surroundings.

Effort: low
Technical detail
  • Reproject each polygon into the UTM zone of every tile it touches. Buffer it by the near-land distance.
  • Intersect with tile footprints from the catalog. Pick the primary tile by the provisional rule.
  • Compute the pixel window on the 10, 20, and 60 m grids of each tile. Store it per polygon version.
2

Describe the pixels we want.

Classify interior, shoreline, and nearby land pixels at each native resolution. Save coverage and edge distance.

Effort: medium
Technical detail
  • Rasterize the polygon onto each grid at native resolution, keeping the area fraction inside the polygon per pixel.
  • Classify interior at fraction 1 and shoreline between 0 and 1. Select near-land at fraction 0 with its centre inside the buffer.
  • Save a mask table per tile and resolution, keyed by polygon version. Recompute only when polygon, grid, or rule changes.
ONCE PER OBSERVATION · MANY TIMES A DAY

Bring in the measurements.

Historical backfill repeats this work across every past date.

3

Find products and read image windows.

Search the catalog, resolve versions, and read the image blocks covering the water bodies.

Effort: high
Technical detail
  • Query the catalog by tile and time range. Keep product id, processing version, file locations, scale, and offset.
  • Treat same-pass splits and reprocessings as separate products. Never merge them.
  • Windowed approach: read internal blocks covering each window through HTTP range requests. Preserve native band grids.
  • Alternatives to compare: one whole-tile read shared by every lake in the tile, and a lazy array stack.
4

Attach quality, values, and source history.

Convert stored values one tested way. Attach quality codes, pixel classes, tile identity, and processing version.

Effort: medium
Technical detail
  • Validate conversion against source values. Keep delivered integers and conversion metadata. Whether to store converted reflectance remains open.
  • Join the mask table: pixel class, coverage fraction, edge distance.
  • Attach the scene class code at 20 m, cloud and snow probability where delivered, product id, version, tile, role, and sensing time.
5

Store records the model can retrieve.

Keep individual band values and quality context. The layout must allow safe re-runs and later queries.

Effort: depends on the layout
Technical detail
  • Make retries duplicate-safe. Preserve published records and their availability times. Store changed polygons or processing results as new revisions.
  • Candidates to compare: Parquet files on object storage partitioned by water body and year. A lakehouse table with atomic commits. A chunked array store. Per-lake raster chips.
  • Set the local publication time on each record, so a query can ask what was known as of a date.

A useful hypothesis: nearby lakes share image blocks. Grouping them may save more than adding compute.

01 / Test cases

Give each method the same difficult cases.

Small ponds, complex shorelines, and contrasting settings help us expose differences before we estimate the cost of serving thousands of water bodies.

Small ponds

The pilot spans @@PILOT_CLASSES@@ m size classes, including @@PILOT_TINY@@ bodies in the smallest class. We still need to count interior and shoreline pixels.

Why it matters: a mapped pond may have no fully contained satellite pixel. Area alone cannot tell us.

Complex shorelines

Tahoe, Lanier, Okeechobee, Grand Lake St. Marys, and Washington contribute larger polygons. Lanier adds a particularly detailed boundary.

Why it matters: shoreline complexity and area can change extraction work. Their effects on runtime have not been measured.

Contrasting settings

The six regions include mountain, hill, flat, and lowland settings. Alaska contributes smaller lakes near Iliamna, without the large lake itself.

Why it matters: these cases support later checks across different conditions. The terrain labels are descriptive, and seasonal imagery has not been tested.

What “10 m” means here. Size labels use the square root of polygon area, not the actual distance across a pond. These mapped boundaries are test inputs. Their shoreline accuracy and water presence on any image date remain unverified.

Documented: USGS no longer maintains NHD. A service refresh does not date the shoreline observations. Some reservoir codes leave their purpose unspecified. Confirm water-body type and boundaries before interpreting extracted pixels. Independent USGS source checks.

Measured: selection and geometry metadata in the manifest. Unverified: current water extent and suitability for the model. This small, deliberately selected set is not a representative workload sample.

03 / Stage 1 · Raw access

Reading whole tiles from both copies.

Before comparing extraction methods, we timed the simplest thing: read every band of one tile from each copy. One acquisition, tile @@RAW_TILE@@ on @@RAW_SCENE_DATE@@, the same ESA product on both copies. Every combination ran three times in a fresh process.

Whole-object reads fetch each file in one request. The quality and all-band sets hold different files on the two copies.
Band setCollection 1 copy · range readsCollection 1 copy · whole objectOlder copy · range readsOlder copy · whole objectPeak memory, highest of both copies and modes

Fewer, larger requests were faster on this connection

For the 10 m, 20 m, and all-band sets, fetching each whole file beat range reads, even though it moved a third more bytes for the overview pyramids. Four requests against sixty-one for the 10 m set. The 60 m sets and the Collection 1 quality layers went the other way by a small margin.

Why it matters: per-request latency on this connection is one explanation consistent with the timings. The run order and the connection were not controlled, so the cause is not isolated. For small windows, range reads are the only option that avoids the whole file.

The two copies are laid out differently

Similar volume, same block size, but the older copy's 10 m bands needed about three times as many range requests and took a quarter longer. One of its 20 m bands uses a different block size and overview count from its siblings.

Why it matters: read cost depends on how each file is arranged internally, and the older copy is less uniform.

Two quality layers are not GeoTIFFs on the older copy

Its catalog entries for cloud and snow probability point at JPEG 2000 files in the ESA-format bucket, outside this GeoTIFF benchmark. The first pass tried them through an unconfigured S3 path and lost twelve runs. Collection 1 serves both as small GeoTIFFs.

Why it matters: Plan B, using the older copy for backfill, has no cloud or snow probability as GeoTIFF. Whether those JPEG 2000 files open without credentials is untested, and most 2022 fallback items lack the assets in the catalog altogether.

The copies are the same picture in different units, on this product. On every compared reflectance file the older copy holds Collection 1's value minus 1,000, the radiometric offset in stored units, with any result at or below the offset clamped to 1. That rule held on every one of 99.8 million pixels checked. Reflectance at or below zero, which occurs over dark water, therefore survives only on Collection 1: 1.7 percent of coastal pixels on this tile. Both catalogs still declare the same offset for both copies, so a reader that trusts the catalog would subtract it twice on the older copy. The scene classification is identical. Aerosol and water vapour sit on a 20 m grid on Collection 1 and on 60 m and 10 m grids on the older copy. One product was checked. Other items and processing versions are stage 5 work.
