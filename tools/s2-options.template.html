01 / DATA

Two public sensors. We start with Sentinel-2.

A satellite measures reflected sunlight in separate wavelength bands. A water-quality model reads those bands for signs of algae, sediment, and dissolved organic matter.

Chlorophyll and cyanobacteriaPigments shape the red and red-edge bands.
Turbidity and sedimentSuspended particles brighten red and near-infrared.
Dissolved organic matterTea-colored water darkens blue and green.
Surface temperatureNeeds a thermal band. Sentinel-2 has none.

The modeling team decides which bands to use. The store keeps every band, so that decision can change later without a new ingestion.

Sentinel-2

OUR CHOICE
Pixel size
10 m in blue, green, red, and near-infrared. Other bands have 20 or 60 m sampling.
Revisit
Nominally five days at the equator with two satellites. Clouds reduce the usable observations.
Red edge
Three red-edge bands at 20 m, plus narrow near-infrared. These add inputs for pigment models.
Consistency
The MSI instrument family across missions. Instrument calibration and ground processing still vary.
Product
Atmospherically corrected surface reflectance. That correction does not remove every effect on water measurements.
Missing
No thermal band. No record before 2015.

Landsat

ALTERNATIVE FOR LATER
Pixel size
30 m for the multispectral bands on Landsat 8 and 9. Fewer samples on a small lake.
Revisit
Landsat 8 and 9 together repeat every eight days. Clouds reduce the usable observations.
Red edge
None.
Consistency
A longer record across several sensor generations. Cross-sensor comparisons need calibration checks.
Product
Surface-reflectance products are available. We did not benchmark them here.
Strengths
Native 100 m thermal bands on Landsat 8/9, delivered at 30 m. The broader Landsat record begins in 1972.

A different product: 30 m HLS. NASA’s Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 places both sensors on a common 30 m grid. It applies its own atmospheric correction and additional cross-sensor adjustments. Our source keeps Sentinel-2’s native 10, 20, and 60 m band grids. Which product the existing model expects is still an open question.

The choice. We prefer Sentinel-2 for its finer visible pixels, frequent revisits, red-edge bands, and consistent instrument family. Landsat remains useful for thermal observations and longer history.

Documented: Copernicus sensor specifications, USGS band specifications, Landsat revisit, and NASA HLS processing. Independently checked 2026-09-17. Band selection depends on the model. The preference comes from project direction. These benchmarks did not test it.

03 / QUALITY

This is where things can go wrong.

A change in a pixel value can come from the water, the atmosphere, or the processing. We need enough context to tell them apart. Six categories cover the main quality risks.

Land leaks into a water pixel.

Small ponds and shorelines produce mixed pixels. A small location error can change how much land enters a measurement.

Our approach: distinguish interior, shoreline, and nearby land. Keep each pixel’s water fraction.

@@RISK_LAND@@

Clear-looking water can be misleading.

Clouds, shadows, haze, sun glint, and nearby bright land can affect the signal. Quality flags help, but they are imperfect.

Our approach: keep the available quality flags and the shoreline context. Test the screening rules.

@@RISK_FLAGS@@

The same lake can arrive twice.

Satellite tiles overlap. Processing can give the same location different values or quality labels in neighboring tiles.

Our approach: keep every tile’s values separately. Measure the differences in the overlap first. Then choose how the model uses competing observations.

@@RISK_TILES@@

A processing change can look like a trend.

Archives mix processing versions and rules that convert stored integers to reflectance. A wrong conversion creates a false change.

Our approach: keep the product history and test the value conversion across versions.

@@RISK_VERSIONS@@

A sharper grid can imply false detail.

Sentinel-2 bands have 10, 20, or 60 m pixels. Enlarging a coarse band does not create finer observations.

Our approach: keep the native band resolution. When an analysis combines grids, make the resampling explicit.

@@RISK_GRID@@

A missing date can be misread.

A gap can mean no coverage, a catalog omission, or invalid band values. A catalog footprint or a clear classification does not certify every band.

Our approach: track coverage and quality separately. Keep the reason for each missing value.

@@RISK_MISSING@@

Documented: Reviewed geospatial evidence and processing practices. Project direction: Data content and provenance. The drawings are schematics. Screening effectiveness and cross-tile differences are unmeasured.

04 / WORKLOAD

Read only what we need, and reuse the work.

We only need the pixels around water bodies. Five steps get us there. Local prototypes timed preparation and extraction. Quality screening and storage still need measurement.

ONCE PER WATER BODY · REDONE WHEN GEOMETRY CHANGES

Prepare the places.

This work repeats when a boundary, grid, or classification rule changes.

1

Locate water bodies on satellite grids.

Find the tiles and image windows that cover each lake and its surroundings.

Effort: varies with geometry
Technical detail
  • Reproject each polygon into the UTM zone of every tile it touches. Buffer it by the nearby-land distance.
  • Use native tile extents for candidates. Treat catalog footprints as coarse hints. The primary-tile rule is not decided yet.
  • Compute the pixel window on the 10, 20, and 60 m grids of each tile. Store it per polygon version.
2

Describe the pixels we want.

Classify interior, shoreline, and nearby-land pixels at each native resolution. Save the water fraction and the edge distance.

Effort: medium
Technical detail
  • Rasterize the polygon onto each grid at native resolution. Keep each pixel’s water fraction.
  • Classify a pixel as interior at fraction 1 and as shoreline between 0 and 1. Select nearby land at fraction 0 with its center inside the buffer.
  • Save a mask table per tile and resolution, keyed by polygon version. Recompute it only after the polygon, grid, or rule changes.
ONCE PER OBSERVATION · MANY TIMES A DAY

Bring in the measurements.

A historical backfill repeats this work for every past date.

3

Find products and read image windows.

Search the catalog, resolve versions, and read the image blocks that cover the water bodies.

Effort: high
Technical detail
  • Query the catalog by tile and time range. Keep product id, processing version, file locations, scale, and offset.
  • Tell datastrips of one acquisition apart from reprocessed versions. Keep the product identity, and keep each contribution separate.
  • Windowed approach: read the internal blocks that cover each window with HTTP range requests. Keep the native band grids.
  • Alternatives to compare: one whole-tile read shared by every lake in the tile, and a lazy array stack.
4

Attach quality, values, and source history.

Convert stored values one tested way. Attach quality codes, pixel classes, tile identity, and processing version.

Effort: medium
Technical detail
  • Validate the conversion against source values. Keep the delivered integers and the conversion metadata. Whether to store converted reflectance is an open question.
  • Join the mask table: pixel class, water fraction, edge distance.
  • Keep the native quality grids and the per-band no-data. Test how they map onto finer pixels before you define a quality join.
5

Store records the model can retrieve.

Keep individual band values and quality context. The layout must allow safe re-runs and later queries.

Effort: depends on the layout
Technical detail
  • Make retries duplicate-safe. Keep published records and their availability times. Store changed polygons or processing results as new revisions.
  • Candidates to compare: Parquet files on object storage partitioned by water body and year. A lakehouse table with atomic commits. A chunked array store. Per-lake raster chips.
  • Set the local publication time on each record, so a query can ask what was known as of a date.

Measured locally: shared open files and cached blocks reduced requests in Stages 3 and 4. The AWS cost saving is unmeasured.

01 / Test cases

Give each method the same difficult cases.

Small ponds, complex shorelines, and contrasting settings help us expose differences before we estimate the cost of serving thousands of water bodies.

Small ponds

The pilot spans the @@PILOT_CLASSES@@ m size classes. The smallest class holds @@PILOT_TINY@@ bodies. Stage 2 counted their pixels. The smallest ponds have no interior pixel at any resolution. They still yield shoreline and nearby-land pixels.

Why it matters: a mapped pond can have no satellite pixel wholly inside it. Area alone does not tell us.

Complex shorelines

Tahoe, Lanier, Okeechobee, Grand Lake St. Marys, and Washington contribute larger polygons. Lanier has a very detailed boundary.

Why it matters: shoreline complexity and area can change extraction work. Stages 2 and 3 timed these polygons. The anchors dominate the bytes and the mask preparation. The results do not separate complexity from area.

Contrasting settings

The six regions include mountain, hill, flat, and lowland settings. Alaska contributes smaller lakes near Iliamna, without the large lake itself.

Why it matters: these cases support later checks across different conditions. The terrain labels are descriptive. Seasonal imagery is untested.

What “10 m” means here. Size labels use the square root of polygon area, not the actual distance across a pond. These mapped boundaries are test inputs. Their shoreline accuracy and water presence on any image date are unverified.

Documented: USGS no longer maintains NHD. A service refresh does not date the shoreline observations. Some reservoir codes do not state the purpose. Before you interpret extracted pixels, confirm the water-body type and boundary. Independent USGS source checks.

Measured: selection and geometry metadata in the manifest. Unverified: current water extent and suitability for the model. This small set follows size-class rules. It is not a representative workload sample.

03 / Stage 1 · Archive access and values

The same product does not guarantee the same numbers.

We read one acquisition from both GeoTIFF copies: tile @@RAW_TILE@@ on @@RAW_SCENE_DATE@@. Access timings and pixel comparisons exposed differences that a catalog check cannot settle.

The older copy changed dark values

On three tested reflectance bands, its integers equal Collection 1’s minus 1,000, clamped to a minimum of 1. That rule held on every pixel valid in both copies.

Why it matters: this conversion loses values at or below zero reflectance. If a reader applies the catalog’s offset again, it subtracts the offset twice. One product does not validate the fallback history.

Quality assets differ

The tested scene classification was identical. Aerosol and water-vapor grids differed. The older copy linked cloud and snow probabilities as JPEG 2000, outside this GeoTIFF benchmark.

Why it matters: the fallback needs explicit rules for missing or differently sampled quality layers.

File layout affects access

The older copy’s 10 m bands needed about three times the range requests for similar bytes. Whole-object reads were faster for several complete band sets on this connection.

Why it matters: request count matters as well as bytes. A whole-tile timing does not predict the cost of a small lake window.

04 / Stage 2 · One lake at a time

Reading a tiny pond still costs an image block.

We tested @@LAKE_LAKES@@ public water bodies across @@LAKE_TILES@@ tiles with four extraction methods. Each lake ran in its own process, three times: @@LAKE_RUNS@@ workers in all.

Bytes do not follow pixel count

Most small lakes with pixels touched one internal block per file. Those reads took 15 requests for five files, whatever the selected pixel count.

Why it matters: block position, compression, and request overhead set the cost of a small-lake read.

Masks keep shoreline context

Raster masks, index lists, and the lazy stack returned identical values for identical selected pixels. They keep the water fraction, the shoreline pixels, and the nearby land. The naive clip selects differently and omits nearby land.

Why it matters: method comparisons need the same output requirements. A faster clip that omits required context does different work.

One tile per region is not enough

Two ponds fell outside their region’s chosen tile. Three larger polygons were only partly inside. Their extracted records cover only the parts inside the selected tile.

Why it matters: tile membership must come from each polygon. Stages 3 and 4 extend this limited first comparison.

Smallest ponds have no interior pixel. All five 10 m size-class ponds still yield shoreline and nearby-land pixels. The size class is the square root of mapped area, not a measured pond diameter.

05 / Stage 3 · Many lakes in one tile

Share open files before reading whole tiles.

We tested @@TILE_LAKES@@ lakes across @@TILE_TILES@@ selected tiles. Three read patterns and four methods ran three times each: @@TILE_RUNS@@ workers. The runs read partial lakes only inside their selected tiles.

Sharing reduced requests

On five tiles matching Stage 2’s acquisitions, shared raster reads used 24–46 percent of the requests. Bytes fell to 49–87 percent. Twenty-one of 26 pond memberships beside anchors needed no extra request.

Why it matters: once a file is shared, a pond beside an anchor often costs no extra request. Neither a pond count nor a pixel count alone predicts transfer.

Whole tiles transferred much more

Whole-tile reads requested 6–32 times the windowed bytes at this pilot’s lake density. One mostly no-data tile compressed especially well, but still needed more bytes than its windows.

Why it matters: windows used fewer bytes on every tested tile. Whether a whole-tile read pays off depends on lake size, shape, position, and shared blocks. No lake count alone decides it.

Chunk behavior needs tuning

The tested lazy recipe requested 1.6–7.1 times the shared raster bytes. A separate compute per lake reread chunks. The mask selections and values still agreed.

Why it matters: chunk size and kept intermediate results can change transfer. This measures one recipe. It does not set a limit for lazy reading.

Catalog outlines are approximate

Selected item footprints have coarse edges. If a reader treats them as precise coverage boundaries, it can omit candidate areas. One acquisition can also hold distinct, overlapping datastrips within a tile.

Implication: keep native tile candidates and complete product identities. Check per-band validity separately.

Classification is not band validity

Each workload contains @@CROSS_NODATA@@ declared no-data entries among @@CROSS_PIXELS@@ selected band-pixel entries. They occur in 10 m bands, despite nonzero selected 20 m classification values.

Implication: the summaries do not say which water or classification cells hold those zeros. Their cause is unknown. A stored zero does not mean zero reflectance.

Preferred observations are undecided

Ambiguous primary-tile roles are unresolved. The run keeps overlapping observations without blending them. It does not choose a storage layout or test whether those observations agree.

Implication: choose overlap behavior with the model’s requirements and matched-location checks. A complete extraction is only part of that decision.

Resource limits and the boundaries of the reviewed run

@@CROSS_RUNS@@ fresh extraction workers completed, across @@CROSS_ACQUISITIONS@@ acquisitions and three repetitions. The medians include every complete repetition.

The guard used a 4 GiB process budget and 1.5 GiB available-memory reserve. It sampled every 0.25 seconds, with a 128 MiB page-out threshold.

Extraction peaked near 1.80 GiB. Selection reached 3.40 GiB and recorded host swapping, without a guard stop. A run that fits on a laptop is not proof of a run without memory pressure.

The settings were fixed: 512 MiB GDAL cache, 2,048-pixel lazy chunks, and four threads. We did not tune the readers.

The measured implementation is version 2. The review corrected how version 3 selects datastrips and handles coarse footprints. The groups selected here did not change. The revised code has local fixture tests and no new public-data run.
